About

I know what it feels like to sit on both sides of the negotiation.

My commercial foundations were forged in hospitality, from prestigious events to Walt Disney World, where standards are visible, service is immediate, and small details have a disproportionate impact on the experience. It taught me early that commercial performance and customer experience are rarely separate problems.

From there, I moved to the buying side before crossing the table into enterprise sales, P&L ownership, client leadership, and company-wide commercial responsibility. That mix means I rarely look at growth purely through a sales lens; I think about what the customer will actually buy, where value is created, what makes a proposition credible, and what has to happen operationally to deliver it.

Curiosity is the thread that connects all of it. AI is now part of how I research, analyse, prototype, automate, and test ideas faster — useful when it removes work or makes something possible, not simply because it carries an AI label.

How I think

Commercial problems rarely start where the KPI shows up.

A conversion problem may start before sales. A forecast problem may really be a progression problem. A people problem may be a system problem. I use three connected lenses before deciding what to fix.

COMMERCIALENGINE
BUYStart outside the organisation

Make the business easier to buy.

Before sales gets involved, the buyer is already deciding whether the problem matters, what good looks like, who is credible, and whether changing feels worth the risk.

Why I see it this way

£90m buyer-side procurement exposure across supplier selection, sourcing, negotiation, and risk.

What I test

TRIGGER — What makes this problem worth solving now?

PROOF — What can a cautious buyer verify without taking your word for it?

CHOICE — Why should they choose you over the safest obvious alternative?

Exploring now

How AI changes discovery, comparison, trust, and shortlisting before the first human conversation.

SELLFollow buyer progress, not seller activity

Make progress something you can prove.

A deal has progressed only when something has changed for the buyer: a problem is clearer, a stakeholder is engaged, a requirement is agreed, risk is reduced, or a commitment is made.

Why I see it this way

£50m+ seller-side P&L ownership, enterprise accounts, pricing, negotiation, and strategic growth.

What I test

PROGRESS — What buyer action proves this deal has actually moved?

CONSENSUS — Who must agree, and what decision does each person own?

RISK — What remains unresolved enough to stop the buyer committing?

BUILDRemove dependence on individual heroics

Make good performance survive the individual.

If success depends on tacit knowledge, heroic effort, or duplicated admin, adding more people and technology can scale the fragility as quickly as the revenue.

Why I see it this way

Forecasting, pricing, CRM change, commercial process, AI propositions, team leadership, and ventures built from scratch.

What I test

REPEATABILITY — What only works because one person knows how?

FORECAST — Can two managers inspect the same evidence and call the same outcome?

AUTOMATION — Which repeated task has technology actually removed?

Pressure test the engine

What feels wrong?

Choose the symptom. I’d answer this question before adding more people, process, or technology.

START WITHSELLProgression

At which buyer decision do otherwise qualified opportunities stop progressing?

This separates a conversion problem from vague “we need more pipeline” thinking. Check the last 10 qualified opportunities and identify the last buyer commitment you can actually evidence.
